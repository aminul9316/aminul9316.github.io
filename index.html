1. Current research

Charge-transfer states of luminescent non-fullerene acceptors

Organic solar cells are low-cost, flexible alternatives to silicon solar cells, and their energy conversion is controlled by charge-transfer (CT) states. My M.Sc. research investigates the excited-state electronic structure and photophysics of luminescent molecules as non-fullerene acceptors (NFAs), focusing on their CT states. I measure absorption and fluorescence in solution and film, and use electroabsorption (EA) and Stark fluorescence (SF) spectroscopy to determine the change in dipole moment (Δμ) and polarizability (Δα) on photoexcitation, together with the relaxation of the excited states by energy or charge transfer.

2. Photophysics

What happens after a molecule absorbs a photon

After absorption, an excited molecule can return to the ground state by emitting light (fluorescence), by converting the energy to heat (internal conversion), by crossing to a triplet state (intersystem crossing), or by transferring an electron to form a charge-transfer state. The balance between these channels decides whether a material is a good emitter, a good light harvester, or neither. Measuring how each excited state responds to an electric field tells us which of these states carries charge-transfer character.

For a candidate acceptor, the useful outcome is a state that separates charge efficiently while losing little energy to heat. Electric-field spectroscopy identifies which states carry that charge-transfer character; fluorescence quantum yields and lifetimes show how fast each loss channel competes.

Key quantities I measure

Δμ
change in dipole moment on excitation; large for charge-transfer states
Δα
change in polarizability; how easily the excited electron cloud deforms
ΦF
fluorescence quantum yield; fraction of excitations emitted as light
τF
fluorescence lifetime; how long the excited state survives
Δν̃
Stokes shift; energy lost to relaxation before emission
3. Methods

Measuring a molecule's response to light and to an electric field

An absorption spectrum shows where a transition occurs. Electric-field spectroscopy shows what kind of transition it is. A field of the order of 0.1–1 MV cm⁻¹ is applied across a thin film of the sample, and the small field-induced change in its absorption (EA) or fluorescence (SF) spectrum is recorded. Fitting these changes with Liptay theory gives Δμ and Δα, for the absorbing Franck–Condon state with EA and for the relaxed emitting state with SF.

Absorption & fluorescence

UV–Vis · PL · solution and film

Steady-state spectra of the NFA candidates in solution and in thin films, including quantum yield and Stokes shift.

Electroabsorption (EA)

field-modulated absorption

Change in absorbance ΔA detected at twice the modulation frequency with a lock-in amplifier. Gives Δμ and Δα of the absorbing state.

Stark fluorescence (SF)

field-modulated emission

Change in fluorescence intensity and shape in a field. Gives Δμ and Δα of the emitting state and reveals field-induced quenching or enhancement.

Excited-state relaxation

energy transfer · charge transfer

Relaxation pathways that decide whether a CT state forms, emits or recombines.

Quantum-chemical calculations

if necessary

Computed excited states and charge distributions to assign the measured transitions.

4. Research interests

Following light-driven charge and energy flow in time

10⁻¹⁸ s

Attosecond science

Attosecond light pulses resolve the motion of electrons themselves. I am interested in charge migration inside molecules, which begins before the nuclei have time to move.

10⁻¹⁵ – 10⁻¹² s

Ultrafast spectroscopy

Femtosecond pump–probe and transient absorption spectroscopy follow excited states as they relax, transfer energy and separate charge, step by step in real time.

10⁻¹³ – 10⁻⁹ s

Charge transfer

Intramolecular and interfacial charge-transfer states in donor–acceptor dyads and organic solar-cell materials: how they form, separate into free charges, or recombine.

10⁻⁹ s

Fluorescence

Steady-state, time-resolved and Stark fluorescence as sensitive probes of excited-state structure, quantum yield and the nonradiative channels that compete with emission.

Ph.D. interests

I am looking for a Ph.D. position in physical chemistry or chemical physics where I can study charge-transfer and energy-transfer processes in real time using ultrafast and attosecond spectroscopy. My M.Sc. research gives me a static picture of charge redistribution in excited molecules through electroabsorption and Stark fluorescence spectroscopy. I want to add the time dimension: to watch charge-transfer states form, separate and decay, and to connect these dynamics to the design of better materials for solar energy conversion.

  • Questions: how fast do CT states form, and what decides whether they separate or recombine?
  • Methods I want to learn: femtosecond transient absorption, time-resolved fluorescence, attosecond pulse techniques
  • What I bring: field-modulated spectroscopy, lock-in detection, quantitative spectral analysis in Python, and four years of laboratory research
